# Equipment Design, Research & Production

## 1. Purpose

This specification defines how national scientific knowledge becomes designed components, integrated equipment systems, tested prototypes, approved production designs, manufactured inventory, and equipment introduced into service.

The system must support a deeply configurable military-industrial simulation. The Player is not limited to researching abstract technology levels and selecting pre-authored equipment. The Player can develop component designs, define their parameters within known engineering constraints, combine components into larger systems, evaluate calculated performance, build prototypes, conduct testing, revise designs, select manufacturers, and commission production.

The system is informed by the component and design depth of Aurora 4X, while remaining grounded in the historical, economic, organisational, and operational scope of Project Sovereign.

The campaign begins on 1 January 1955. Research, industrial development, and equipment design should support historical progression through at least 2026, with an extensible framework for later developments if the simulation permits.

## 2. Core principles

1. **Technology is knowledge, not a finished product.** Research unlocks principles, methods, materials, processes, and design options. It does not automatically create a fielded weapon or upgrade existing equipment.
2. **Design is player-authored.** The Player can define and name components and complete equipment designs, subject to available knowledge, engineering constraints, resources, facilities, and time.
3. **Components and complete systems are distinct.** Engines, sensors, fire-control systems, weapons, power plants, transmissions, avionics, and other components can be designed independently and integrated into larger systems.
4. **Performance is calculated.** Design outputs emerge from component characteristics, configuration, mass, geometry, power, heat, fuel, drag, structural limits, and other relevant constraints. Avoid arbitrary universal bonuses or a single technology score standing in for engineering.
5. **Prototypes are not production items.** A design may be plausible on paper but fail integration, testing, reliability, safety, or manufacturing requirements.
6. **Design authority and manufacturing are separate.** The Player or national design organisation owns the design decision; relevant industrial companies manufacture it using their actual capabilities and capacity.
7. **Manufacturing is economic activity.** Production consumes materials, components, labour, energy, tooling capacity, factory time, capital, and supplier capacity, and is constrained by delivery schedules and bottlenecks.
8. **A design may be producible without being service-ready.** Adoption requires acceptance, training, maintenance arrangements, spares, ammunition or fuel compatibility, doctrine, and logistical support as appropriate.
9. **Variants preserve design history.** Revisions and upgrades create identifiable versions. Existing equipment does not silently change when a new design is approved.
10. **National capability is uneven.** Countries differ in research, industrial depth, design institutions, manufacturing quality, supply chains, capital, and access to foreign knowledge.
11. **Historical plausibility should coexist with sandbox freedom.** The simulation should allow plausible counterfactual development while respecting prerequisites, costs, technical limits, and time.
12. **Uncertainty is explicit.** Early estimates can be uncertain; prototypes and trials reduce uncertainty. The simulation should distinguish calculated estimates, observed test results, and actual production performance.

## 3. Conceptual lifecycle

Equipment development is a connected lifecycle rather than a single research action.

1. Discover or acquire the relevant scientific and engineering knowledge.
2. Establish a design programme and its objectives.
3. Develop component designs.
4. Integrate compatible components into an equipment design.
5. Calculate preliminary performance and identify constraints.
6. Build prototypes or test articles.
7. Test, evaluate, and revise the design.
8. Decide whether to cancel, continue development, license, or adopt.
9. Qualify the design for production and establish manufacturing arrangements.
10. Contract one or more qualified companies.
11. Manufacture, inspect, accept, and deliver equipment.
12. Introduce the equipment into service and sustain it.
13. Develop variants, improvements, replacement designs, or derivative systems.

Not every programme requires every stage to the same degree. A simple component based on mature technology may need limited testing; a new aircraft, submarine, missile, or nuclear-capable system may require extensive integrated trials and certification.

## 4. Research and technological knowledge

### 4.1 Research is a prerequisite system

Research should represent accumulated national knowledge, not a generic level that directly determines every product statistic. Research topics may include:

- Scientific principles and physical effects.
- Engineering methods and design practices.
- Materials and material treatments.
- Manufacturing processes and tolerances.
- Component architectures.
- Testing and measurement methods.
- Reliability engineering and quality control.
- Integration methods and systems engineering.
- Operational concepts that create new equipment requirements.

Research topics can have prerequisites, parallel dependencies, maturity stages, and different applications. For example, advances in metallurgy may improve turbine temperature tolerance, while advances in aerodynamics and control systems may independently affect aircraft performance.

### 4.2 Knowledge states

A research topic may progress through states such as:

- **Unknown:** the country has no established access to the knowledge.
- **Under investigation:** a research programme is active.
- **Established:** the core principles are understood.
- **Engineering application:** the country can apply the knowledge in a design process.
- **Mature:** the knowledge is well understood and can be applied with lower uncertainty and risk.

These are conceptual states, not mandatory universal tiers for every topic. Some topics may require numeric progress, separate subtopics, or discrete discoveries.

Research completion should not imply that every industry, company, or design team can immediately use the knowledge. The ability to apply it may depend on trained personnel, institutions, equipment, facilities, documentation, tacit knowledge, and industrial experience.

### 4.3 Research organisations and allocation

National research capacity may be distributed among government laboratories, military establishments, universities, state-owned enterprises, private companies, and joint programmes.

The Player should be able to allocate funding, staff, facilities, and priorities where the simulation models them. Research output depends on relevant expertise, tools, funding, institutional efficiency, collaboration, and programme management. Additional spending may yield diminishing returns where the limiting factor is specialist staff, test facilities, or unavailable materials.

### 4.4 Research uncertainty and failure

Research programmes can be delayed, redirected, or unsuccessful. Uncertainty should be meaningful but not arbitrarily punitive. A programme can establish useful knowledge even if its intended application fails. Results may also reveal that a theoretical approach is less practical than expected.

### 4.5 Historical starting point and extensibility

On 1 January 1955, national knowledge, designs, industrial facilities, and production capabilities should reflect the selected historical scenario. Research can progress through the historical period to at least 2026. Later technologies should be extensible through data and prerequisites rather than requiring a redesign of the system.

The model should distinguish a technology's historical discovery from its adoption by a particular country or company. A technology existing somewhere in the world does not mean every country can use it immediately.

## 5. Design programmes and design authority

A design programme is an organised effort to create or improve a component or complete system. It has an objective, responsible organisation, resources, schedule, prerequisites, target requirements, and development status.

A programme may be initiated by the Player, a military service, a government agency, a research organisation, or a company acting under delegated authority. The Player's control over exact design parameters may depend on national institutions and scenario rules, but the design system should support direct Player authorship where intended.

The programme should define:

- Intended role and operational requirements.
- Target performance and acceptable trade-offs.
- Required components and technologies.
- Design authority and participating organisations.
- Budget, staffing, facilities, and expected schedule.
- Prototype and test strategy.
- Intended manufacturers or industrial partners, if known.
- Security, export, licensing, and classification constraints where modelled.

Requirements should guide design and evaluation without guaranteeing that a design can meet them. Competing requirements may be mutually incompatible or require unacceptable cost, mass, size, complexity, or risk.

## 6. Component design system

### 6.1 Components are independently identifiable designs

The Player should be able to create and name component designs, then reuse compatible designs in multiple parent systems. Components may include, but are not limited to:

- Jet engines, piston engines, turboprops, rocket motors, and other propulsion systems.
- Generators, batteries, power distribution, and auxiliary power units.
- Radars, sonars, optical sensors, electronic support measures, and other detection systems.
- Fire-control computers, navigation systems, communications equipment, and avionics.
- Guns, missiles, torpedoes, launchers, and other armament.
- Transmissions, suspensions, tracks, wheels, and vehicle powertrains.
- Armour packages, structural assemblies, and protective systems.
- Pumps, compressors, cooling systems, environmental control, and life-support equipment.
- Ship machinery, propulsion shafts, boilers, turbines, and auxiliary systems.
- Computers, processors, data links, and control systems.
- Materials, subassemblies, and specialised manufacturing processes where useful.

The system should support component categories and extensible schemas rather than relying on a closed list of hard-coded equipment types.

### 6.2 Parameters depend on component class

There should be no single universal component editor. Each class exposes parameters relevant to its function, while shared fields enable comparison and integration.

A jet engine design may include, where modelled:

- Engine architecture and cycle.
- Thrust at specified operating conditions.
- Specific fuel consumption or equivalent efficiency measures.
- Dry and afterburning thrust, if applicable.
- Mass, dimensions, and installation envelope.
- Airflow, pressure ratio, turbine inlet temperature, and other design parameters.
- Fuel and lubricant requirements.
- Cooling, electrical, and bleed-air demands.
- Operating limits and environmental tolerances.
- Expected service life, inspection intervals, and maintenance burden.
- Development maturity, reliability estimates, and manufacturing difficulty.

A radar may expose frequency band, power, antenna characteristics, detection performance assumptions, resolution, tracking capacity, electrical load, cooling needs, mass, and reliability. A gun may expose calibre, barrel length, ammunition compatibility, rate of fire, recoil, mass, wear, and mounting requirements.

Parameter sets should be domain-specific and data-driven. The simulation should avoid presenting false precision where the underlying model cannot support it.

### 6.3 Design constraints and trade-offs

The Player should make meaningful engineering choices. Improving one characteristic may worsen another. Examples include:

- Higher thrust may increase engine mass, fuel consumption, heat, cost, or maintenance.
- Greater radar power may increase detection potential while increasing electrical demand, cooling burden, emissions, and equipment size.
- More armour may improve protection while increasing mass and reducing mobility or payload.
- Greater gun performance may increase recoil, barrel wear, ammunition mass, or mounting requirements.
- Greater reliability may require conservative operating limits, improved materials, tighter tolerances, or more expensive manufacturing.
- Miniaturisation may require more advanced materials, manufacturing precision, thermal management, and research.

Trade-offs should be derived from component-specific rules and equations where feasible, not applied as arbitrary global penalties.

### 6.4 Design names and identities

Every design should have a stable identity, player-authored name, design lineage, version, creator or design authority, and development history.

Names are not technical specifications. The simulation must maintain machine-readable parameters separately from display names. Renaming a design should not accidentally create a new technical design unless the Player explicitly chooses to fork or revise it.

### 6.5 Design versions and branches

When the Player changes a design after testing or production, the system should preserve prior versions. A revision can be treated as:

- A new version within the same design lineage.
- A derivative or variant based on an earlier design.
- A substantially new design with shared components or ancestry.

The correct choice depends on how extensive the change is and how the simulation represents compatibility, certification, tooling, and logistics. A minor avionics change may preserve most of an aircraft's identity; a new engine, altered structure, or changed weapon integration may require substantial requalification.

## 7. Complete equipment design and integration

### 7.1 Parent designs and component slots

A complete equipment design is an assembly of components, structure, and configuration. It defines component slots, quantities, installation locations, interfaces, and other constraints appropriate to its class.

Examples include:

- Aircraft composed of airframe, one or more engines, fuel systems, avionics, sensors, armaments, and payloads.
- Ground vehicles composed of hull, powertrain, suspension, armour, weapons, sights, communications, and crew arrangements.
- Warships composed of hull, propulsion, power generation, sensors, combat systems, weapons, and internal spaces.
- Missiles composed of propulsion, guidance, control, warhead, fuze, and other relevant subsystems.
- Radar installations composed of antenna, transmitter, receiver, processing, power, cooling, and communications components.

The design interface should allow the Player to select, replace, configure, and quantity-adjust components subject to technical constraints. Where meaningful, the Player should also configure placement and layout, not merely choose from fixed package names.

### 7.2 Compatibility and interfaces

A component can only be installed where relevant interfaces and constraints are satisfied. Compatibility may include:

- Physical dimensions, mounting points, and available volume.
- Mass, centre of gravity, and structural load.
- Electrical generation, voltage, power draw, and distribution.
- Heat generation and cooling capacity.
- Fuel, lubricant, hydraulic, pneumatic, or other service requirements.
- Data interfaces, signal compatibility, and software or control requirements.
- Weapon, ammunition, launcher, and fire-control compatibility.
- Environmental and pressure limits.
- Crew, maintenance, and access requirements.
- Safety, certification, and security constraints.

Compatibility should be explicit. A component should not become compatible merely because the Player has researched it. Adapters or redesigns may resolve some incompatibilities, but they can add mass, cost, complexity, failure modes, and development time.

### 7.3 Multiplicity and configuration

The Player must be able to install multiple instances of a component where the parent design permits it. For example, an aircraft design may use two copies of a selected jet engine. The simulation should calculate the consequences of the actual configuration, including combined thrust, fuel demand, engine mass, installation drag, nacelle geometry, structural reinforcement, redundancy, and failure implications.

Two engines should not automatically mean exactly twice the aircraft's speed, range, or overall performance. Aircraft performance emerges from the complete design and operating conditions.

### 7.4 Calculated performance

The equipment designer should provide live or on-demand calculated outputs as components and parameters change. Outputs should include relevant metrics for the design class.

For an aircraft, these may include:

- Empty, operating, and maximum take-off mass.
- Internal fuel capacity and fuel fraction.
- Payload capacity and station limits.
- Maximum speed and cruise speed at defined altitudes and conditions.
- Range and endurance under stated profiles.
- Climb performance and ceiling.
- Take-off and landing requirements.
- Engine-out or degraded performance where modelled.
- Electrical and cooling margins.
- Drag, structural limits, and centre-of-gravity envelope.
- Maintenance demand, reliability estimate, acquisition cost, and operating cost.

For a ground vehicle, outputs may include mass, power-to-weight ratio, speed, range, ground pressure, obstacle capability, protection, crew, ammunition capacity, and maintainability. For a ship, outputs may include displacement, speed, range, endurance, stability or loading margins, power availability, sensor and weapon fit, and complement.

The interface should show assumptions and relevant operating conditions. Maximum speed without altitude or configuration assumptions can be misleading; range without a defined profile and reserve policy is similarly incomplete.

### 7.5 Constraint feedback

The design interface should explain why a design is invalid, marginal, or expensive. Examples include excessive mass, inadequate thrust, insufficient cooling, incompatible data interfaces, insufficient electrical power, overloaded structure, or a component that exceeds its installation envelope.

Where feasible, show the binding constraint and likely remedies. The Player should be able to compare the effects of changes before committing to a prototype.

## 8. Prototypes and testing

### 8.1 Prototype distinction

A prototype is a physical or simulated test article built to evaluate a design or subsystem. It is not equivalent to a production-ready item and does not automatically enter national inventory.

Prototypes can be component-level, subsystem-level, or full-system. A new engine may be bench-tested before being installed in an aircraft testbed; the aircraft then requires its own integration and flight-test programme.

### 8.2 Prototype resources

Prototyping may consume:

- Prototype-specific components and materials.
- Skilled engineering and workshop labour.
- Test facilities, ranges, instrumentation, and test aircraft or platforms.
- Time, funding, and access to specialist suppliers.
- Fuel, ammunition, test articles, and other consumables.
- Test crews and safety support.

Some prototype articles may be recoverable, reusable, or convertible to later uses; others may be consumed, damaged, or rendered unsuitable for operational service.

### 8.3 Test programmes

Test plans should reflect the type and novelty of the design. Relevant activities can include bench testing, environmental testing, stress and fatigue testing, compatibility testing, static trials, flight or sea trials, firing trials, reliability trials, and operational evaluation.

Testing can discover:

- Performance shortfalls.
- Reliability and durability problems.
- Thermal, structural, or vibration issues.
- Compatibility failures.
- Safety risks.
- Maintenance difficulties.
- Manufacturing defects or impractical tolerances.
- Unexpected performance under real operating conditions.

### 8.4 Test results and uncertainty

The system should distinguish predicted performance from measured performance. Test quality depends on the instrumentation, test conditions, sample size, staff expertise, and breadth of the programme.

Tests can increase confidence in performance estimates and reliability. They may also reveal that the underlying model or design assumptions were wrong. A successful single test does not establish long-term reliability.

### 8.5 Design iteration and cancellation

The Player may revise a design, conduct further tests, reduce requirements, change components, delay the programme, or cancel it. Failed programmes should still have a historical record and may contribute reusable knowledge, data, tooling, or components.

A prototype that meets its target performance may still be rejected if it is too expensive, difficult to manufacture, unreliable, unsafe, or unsustainable in service.

## 9. Production qualification and introduction into service

Production qualification is a distinct decision from technical testing. A design may function correctly but be too difficult or costly to manufacture consistently at scale.

Before production approval, the programme may require:

- Completion of required technical and safety tests.
- Defined and controlled design documentation.
- Manufacturing drawings, tolerances, and process specifications.
- Qualified suppliers and materials.
- Production tooling and inspection procedures.
- Demonstrated or estimated production yield.
- Maintenance manuals, training plans, and spare-parts requirements.
- Approved ammunition, fuel, software, and support interfaces.
- Procurement authority, funding, and acceptance criteria.

The Player should be able to approve a design for limited production, initial operational use, or full-rate production where those distinctions are meaningful. Some equipment can enter service while development continues, but this should be represented as an explicit risk or programme state rather than assumed to be complete.

## 10. Industrial companies and manufacturing

### 10.1 Design ownership versus production

The Player may design a component and assign its manufacture to an appropriate company. The company is the producer, not necessarily the originator of the design.

For example, the Player creates an engine design called **Project Falcon** and awards a production contract to General Electric. The manufactured product may be displayed as **General Electric Project Falcon**, while the underlying design retains its stable identity, author, parameters, version, and lineage.

The naming convention is a display and attribution rule. It must not collapse the Player-authored design into the manufacturer's company identity or imply that the manufacturer independently invented it.

### 10.2 Company capabilities

Companies have differentiated capabilities. These may include:

- Product and component specialisations.
- Manufacturing processes and certifications.
- Factory locations and facility capacity.
- Machine tools, production lines, tooling, and test equipment.
- Skilled workforce and engineering staff.
- Supplier relationships and access to materials.
- Quality systems, yield, reliability, and process maturity.
- Financial health, available capital, and ability to expand.
- Existing commitments and production backlog.
- Security clearance, licensing, and export eligibility.
- Experience with the specific design or related products.

A company should not be able to manufacture every component simply because it has unused factory capacity. Relevant process capability, facilities, tooling, documentation, skilled labour, and supply access are prerequisites.

### 10.3 Manufacturing arrangements

The Player may be able to choose among eligible arrangements:

- Manufacture by a state-owned or national company.
- Contract a domestic private company.
- License production to a domestic or foreign company.
- Co-produce across multiple companies or countries.
- Import completed equipment or subassemblies.
- Establish a new facility or expand an existing one.
- Build under foreign technical assistance, where permitted.

These arrangements differ in cost, speed, domestic industrial development, security exposure, intellectual-property control, foreign dependency, and production resilience.

### 10.4 Manufacturer attribution and designation

The simulation should preserve separate identities for:

- The underlying component or equipment design.
- Its designer or design authority.
- The manufacturer or manufacturing licensee.
- The production version and applicable configuration.
- The manufacturing site and production lot.
- The service designation, if different from the design or manufacturer's name.

A design may be produced by more than one company. Different manufacturers may deliver the same nominal design with different quality, reliability, cost, or delivery outcomes if their processes and quality systems differ. Where manufacturing differences materially change performance or interchangeability, those differences should be tracked as production variants or configuration changes.

## 11. Production economics and industrial capacity

### 11.1 Production orders

A production order defines the quantity, specification, manufacturer, delivery schedule, destination, contract terms, and acceptance requirements for an item or batch.

The production system should track order state, such as planned, contracted, awaiting inputs, in production, delayed, awaiting inspection, delivered, rejected, or cancelled.

### 11.2 Production inputs

Manufacturing may consume:

- Raw materials and refined commodities.
- Standard and specialised components.
- Energy and industrial services.
- Skilled and general labour.
- Machine time and facility capacity.
- Tooling, jigs, dies, moulds, and test equipment.
- Production engineering and quality assurance.
- Capital expenditure and working capital.
- Licensed designs, process knowledge, and supplier services.

Input requirements should be linked to the design's bill of materials and manufacturing process where the model supports that level of detail.

### 11.3 Capacity, throughput, and bottlenecks

Production throughput is constrained by the full manufacturing chain, not only the final assembly line. A factory may have spare assembly capacity but be unable to increase output due to a shortage of engines, precision castings, semiconductors, skilled labour, or test capacity.

The system should model bottlenecks at an appropriate scale, including supplier dependencies and shared industrial capacity. Expanding production may require capital expenditure, construction time, hiring, training, tooling, qualification, and additional resource supply.

### 11.4 Costs and delivery

Costs can include non-recurring engineering, tooling, prototype expenditure, unit production costs, licensing, imported inputs, quality assurance, transport, acceptance, and ongoing support.

Unit costs may change with production volume, learning effects, material prices, labour costs, process yield, supplier capacity, and design maturity. The model should not assume unlimited economies of scale: shortages, overtime, congestion, quality degradation, or scarce inputs may increase marginal cost.

Delivery dates should be estimates that respond to available inputs and capacity. Delays should have causes the Player can inspect rather than appearing as unexplained random outcomes.

### 11.5 Manufacturing quality and yield

Production does not guarantee that every unit meets specification. Relevant concepts include:

- First-pass yield and rework.
- Inspection and acceptance rates.
- Manufacturing defects.
- Process consistency and tolerance control.
- Component reliability and expected service life.
- Supplier quality and traceability.
- Lot-to-lot variation.

Quality outcomes should be influenced by design maturity, process maturity, tooling, workforce skills, supplier quality, inspection investment, and production pressure. Avoid excessive randomness where a deterministic or seeded model with clear causal factors is possible.

### 11.6 National industrial development

The Player may invest in industrial capacity, workforce development, supplier networks, tooling, research facilities, or production processes. Such investments can have broader economic effects and may improve a country's ability to develop future equipment.

Industrial capacity should be reusable across programmes where technically appropriate, but not universally interchangeable. A precision turbine manufacturing line and a basic vehicle assembly line are not equivalent capacity.

## 12. Procurement, inventory, and service introduction

Production output must connect to the Equipment & Materiel System. Manufactured units are not automatically considered operationally available simply because they leave the factory.

The delivery and acceptance workflow may include:

1. Completion at the manufacturing site.
2. Factory testing and quality inspection.
3. Government or military acceptance.
4. Transport to a depot, unit, or installation.
5. Registration as equipment instances or production lots.
6. Crew or operator conversion training.
7. Establishment of maintenance, spares, fuel, ammunition, and support.
8. Assignment to a formation or service.
9. Readiness certification for the intended role.

The precise sequence varies by equipment class. Equipment may be accepted with limitations, held in reserve, stored, sent for further testing, or rejected.

A new design can require new training, maintenance tooling, spare parts, technical manuals, facilities, and doctrine. The cost of adoption therefore extends beyond the acquisition price.

## 13. Variants, upgrades, and obsolescence

### 13.1 Variants

A variant is a controlled derivative of an existing design. Variants can reflect different mission roles, export customers, national requirements, manufacturers, or operational environments.

The system should preserve ancestry and identify which components and interfaces are shared. A variant may require its own prototype programme, qualification, documentation, production tooling, and logistics arrangements.

### 13.2 Upgrades

Upgrades can replace components, change software, improve materials, modify structure, or revise production processes. The effect depends on the scope of the change.

Upgrades may be applied to existing equipment only when compatible and when the necessary parts, labour, facilities, downtime, and funding are available. A new research discovery or approved design must not automatically retrofit existing inventory.

### 13.3 Obsolescence

Equipment can become obsolete or uneconomic due to changing operational requirements, new threats, unavailable parts, industrial discontinuation, rising maintenance costs, or superior alternatives.

Obsolescence should be a decision and assessment, not a binary technology-age flag. Older equipment may remain useful in secondary roles, reserves, training, export, or lower-threat environments.

## 14. Technology access, diffusion, and security

### 14.1 Access routes

A country may gain technology or design access through domestic research, purchase, licensing, technical cooperation, alliances, industrial partnerships, captured equipment, intelligence collection, or reverse engineering.

Access to a physical example is not equivalent to access to complete design documentation, manufacturing know-how, source code, specialised materials, or a qualified supply chain.

### 14.2 Licensing and production rights

A licence can grant rights to use, modify, manufacture, or export a design under defined conditions. Rights may be limited by quantity, territory, customer, component source, or time. Licensing can reduce development time but introduce royalties, dependencies, or restrictions.

### 14.3 Export controls and restrictions

Where modelled, exports can be restricted by national policy, treaty, security classification, supplier conditions, or political relationships. Restrictions may apply separately to complete systems, components, production tooling, technical data, software, and manufacturing assistance.

### 14.4 Reverse engineering and captured equipment

Captured or imported equipment can provide a basis for technical investigation. Reverse engineering outcomes depend on condition, completeness, available expertise, tools, test facilities, and time. It may reveal design principles without reproducing the original manufacturing process or performance.

Reverse engineering should not automatically grant the complete technology tree, exact design files, or immediate mass-production capability.

### 14.5 Technology diffusion

Knowledge may diffuse through personnel movement, industrial collaboration, publications, espionage, exports, licensing, and observation of deployed equipment. Diffusion can be incomplete, delayed, contested, or restricted. The simulation should track the distinction between knowing a principle, possessing a design, and being able to manufacture it reliably.

## 15. Company and programme interaction

Design programmes and manufacturing companies should interact through explicit relationships rather than implicit bonuses.

A company may contribute engineering expertise, propose modifications, perform integration work, build prototypes, or develop its own competing design. The Player may delegate part of the programme, retain design authority, or negotiate the scope of a contract.

Company incentives can affect cost, delivery, investment, and willingness to participate. Financial distress, competing contracts, labour shortages, or political priorities may affect execution. Where company autonomy is simulated, the Player should still be able to understand the source of delays and negotiate or change arrangements where possible.

A manufacturer may improve its processes through repeated production, but production experience should not instantly grant unrelated design expertise. Manufacturing learning, engineering design capability, and research capability are related but distinct.

## 16. User interface requirements

The design and production interface should support the following workflows.

### 16.1 Research overview

The shared knowledge/unlock boundary is specified in [Research and Technology Progression Interface](../../technical/architecture/research-technology-progression-interface.md) (RES-IF-001). That interface is supporting architecture only; the full Research System remains to be authored after Military integration dependencies are closed.

Show available research topics, prerequisites, progress, cost, expected completion, responsible institutions, and potential applications. Clearly distinguish research that is complete from capabilities that are actually available for use.

### 16.2 Component designer

Allow the Player to select a component class, define parameters, view constraints, name and save designs, compare alternatives, inspect dependencies, and preserve versions.

### 16.3 Complete equipment designer

Provide a component-and-configuration workspace with editable slots, quantities, compatibility feedback, calculated performance, warnings, and comparisons. The Player should be able to inspect why a particular design performs as it does.

### 16.4 Prototype and test programme

Show objectives, test plans, resource requirements, progress, test conditions, observed results, failures, uncertainties, and recommended design changes. Recommendations should explain their basis and must not silently modify the design.

### 16.5 Production planning

Show eligible manufacturers, their relevant capabilities, capacity, current commitments, tooling requirements, suppliers, quoted or estimated costs, delivery schedules, contract terms, and risks.

### 16.6 Fleet and service introduction

Show contracted, in-production, delivered, accepted, rejected, and fielded quantities; equipment version and manufacturer; training and support requirements; and the readiness consequences of adoption.

### 16.7 Comparison and traceability

The Player should be able to compare designs and variants side by side, including performance, cost, manufacturability, reliability, support burden, and development risk. Every calculated output should be traceable to the design configuration and assumptions that produced it.

## 17. Conceptual data model

The following entities are conceptual and may be divided or combined during implementation.

- **TechnologyTopic:** research prerequisites, maturity, knowledge state, and applications.
- **ResearchProgramme:** scope, funding, staffing, progress, outputs, and history.
- **DesignProgramme:** objective, requirements, responsible authority, schedule, and state.
- **ComponentClass:** category, applicable parameters, interfaces, and constraints.
- **ComponentDesign:** identity, name, version, parameters, dependencies, author, and lineage.
- **EquipmentClass:** parent system category and applicable design rules.
- **EquipmentDesign:** parent configuration, component slots, quantities, geometry, and calculated performance.
- **DesignRevision:** versioned changes, rationale, compatibility impact, and approval state.
- **PrototypeArticle:** physical or simulated test article, configuration, build record, and condition.
- **TestProgramme:** planned tests, facilities, methods, results, uncertainty, and evaluation.
- **ProductionQualification:** manufacturing readiness, process documentation, and approval state.
- **ManufacturingCompany:** specialisations, facilities, workforce, capacity, financial condition, and supplier relationships.
- **ManufacturingFacility:** location, processes, tooling, capacity, and constraints.
- **ManufacturingProcess:** required inputs, process steps, yield, quality requirements, and time.
- **ProductionContract:** design/version, manufacturer, quantities, schedule, terms, and acceptance criteria.
- **ProductionOrder:** active execution state, inputs, progress, bottlenecks, and delivery records.
- **ProductionLot:** produced batch, facility, date, configuration, quality record, and traceability.
- **TechnologyAccessRecord:** licensing, transfer, capture, export, reverse engineering, or other access route.
- **ServiceIntroductionRecord:** acceptance, training, logistics, operational approval, and restrictions.

These entities must connect to the existing Equipment & Materiel System. Avoid duplicate representations of equipment inventory, company finances, economic resources, or unit assignments.

## 18. System interfaces

### 18.1 Research and national technology

Consumes and produces research knowledge, institutional capability, specialist personnel, and design prerequisites. Research results must remain distinct from a fielded equipment design.

### 18.2 Equipment & Materiel System

Receives approved designs, versions, component definitions, compatibility information, and production outputs. Maintains equipment instances, condition, serviceability, allocations, ammunition, spares, and inventory.

### 18.3 Economy and industry

Consumes material, labour, energy, capital, industrial capacity, and company resources. Receives production demand and generates costs, output, delays, and investment requirements. Economic and company records remain authoritative in their respective systems.

### 18.4 Military organisation and operations

Provides design requirements and receives fielded equipment, readiness restrictions, training needs, support demands, and operational availability. Research completion alone must not alter unit capability.

### 18.5 Logistics and infrastructure

Connects manufacturing sites, suppliers, depots, ports, railways, roads, and end users. Transport, storage, infrastructure capacity, and disruption can affect delivery and sustainment.

### 18.6 Intelligence and foreign relations

May provide information about foreign research, programmes, production, exports, and captured systems. Information can be incomplete or uncertain; the Player should not necessarily know exact foreign parameters or production rates.

### 18.7 Sensors, electronic warfare, and combat

Provides the technical definitions and performance inputs required by sensor, EW, and combat models. Those systems should consume approved component and equipment characteristics rather than maintain separate, conflicting equipment specifications.

## 19. Simulation integrity and implementation principles

- Keep designs, design versions, prototypes, production orders, production lots, and fielded equipment as distinct states or records.
- Use stable identifiers; display names must not be used as database identity.
- Store source parameters and derived outputs separately. Derived outputs should be recalculated when relevant inputs change.
- Record the design version used by each prototype, production lot, and fielded equipment instance.
- Use explicit compatibility rules and report invalid or marginal configurations.
- Where performance is approximated, document assumptions and avoid implying unsupported engineering precision.
- Keep research knowledge, design capability, manufacturing capability, and operational availability distinct.
- Use deterministic or reproducible calculations where feasible so that identical inputs and assumptions produce consistent results.
- Represent uncertainty and random variation explicitly, with identifiable causes and auditable results.
- Avoid a universal technology multiplier that replaces component-level engineering.
- Avoid automatic upgrading or re-equipping of existing units when research or a design is completed.
- Keep costs and production times connected to economic and industrial systems rather than hard-coded purchase abstractions.
- Preserve historical scenario data separately from simulation rules so that alternate histories remain possible.
- Make component classes, parameter sets, constraints, and manufacturing rules extensible through data where practical.

## 20. Acceptance criteria

The specification is satisfied when the system can support the following outcomes:

1. The Player can research a prerequisite technology without automatically receiving a completed equipment design.
2. The Player can create and name a component design with parameters appropriate to its class.
3. A component design can be reused in multiple parent designs when compatibility permits.
4. The Player can configure a complete equipment design from component selections and quantities.
5. Installing two engines in an aircraft produces calculated results based on the full aircraft configuration rather than a naive doubling of performance.
6. The design interface identifies incompatible components, violated constraints, and relevant trade-offs.
7. Predicted performance is distinguishable from measured prototype performance.
8. The Player can build prototypes, run tests, receive results, revise a design, and preserve prior versions.
9. A successful prototype does not automatically imply mass-production readiness.
10. The Player can qualify a design for production and select an eligible manufacturing company.
11. The company must possess relevant manufacturing capabilities, facilities, tooling, staff, inputs, and capacity or invest to acquire them.
12. A Player-authored design retains its identity when manufactured by a company; manufacturer attribution is represented separately.
13. Production consumes relevant resources, labour, capacity, and time, and can be delayed by identifiable bottlenecks.
14. Production lots retain their configuration, manufacturer, facility, date, and quality history.
15. Delivered equipment enters the Equipment & Materiel System through acceptance and delivery processes rather than appearing instantly in fielded units.
16. Service introduction accounts for relevant training, maintenance, spares, fuel, ammunition, infrastructure, and readiness requirements.
17. New technology or design approval does not automatically upgrade existing inventory.
18. Licensing, foreign access, capture, and reverse engineering do not automatically confer full manufacturing capability.
19. Research and industrial development can support historically plausible progression from 1955 through at least 2026.
20. Calculated outputs, costs, production schedules, and test outcomes are traceable to documented inputs and assumptions.

## 21. Open design decisions

These points should be resolved during implementation or detailed domain modelling rather than assumed prematurely:

- The exact engineering fidelity and calculation model for each equipment class.
- How much geometry and physical layout the interface exposes versus abstracting into constrained design parameters.
- Which materials, manufacturing processes, supply-chain tiers, and quality metrics are simulated explicitly.
- How national research organisations, private-company research, and military design authorities divide responsibilities.
- Whether and how companies can initiate independent designs or propose alternatives to Player requirements.
- How to balance hidden foreign capabilities with intelligence estimates and observed evidence.
- The level of detail used for prototype damage, accidents, test safety, and programme failure.
- How to represent classified designs, export restrictions, sanctions, licensing disputes, and intellectual-property rights.
- The boundary between a minor design revision, a new variant, and a new equipment family.
- Which design classes require detailed physical equations and which use calibrated engineering approximations.

These decisions should be made consistently across aircraft, land systems, naval systems, missiles, sensors, electronic warfare equipment, and later strategic systems. The architecture should permit deeper modelling for selected classes without forcing every component to use identical equations.

## 22. Summary

Equipment development in Project Sovereign is a continuous chain from knowledge to design, integration, testing, production, delivery, and service. The Player authors and configures designs; companies manufacture them within real industrial constraints; and military organisations must adopt and sustain the resulting equipment. Research, engineering, industrial production, inventory, and operational effectiveness remain connected but distinct systems.
